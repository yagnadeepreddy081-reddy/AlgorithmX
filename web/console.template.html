How to read this screen

One sentence first: a radar satellite photographs the sea, this system finds the oil in that photo, works out where the oil was dumped several hours earlier, and then checks which ship was at that spot at that time.

1 · The left column — which scene you are looking at

Three walkthrough scenes are the ones to present. Below them is a test set of 38 scenes nobody chose — random weather, random ship courses, and eight scenes with no oil in them at all. Press Random scene and one loads. Every result is kept, including the failures, and the page tells you which is which.

2 · The middle — the map

The grey square is a generated radar-like image, with an assumed width of 41 km. Dark patches are calm or oily water; bright dots are ships. The orange shape is what the model decided is oil. The amber dashed shapes are dark patches the system looked at and rejected as not-oil. The blue line is a ship's AIS track; grey lines are ships that were ruled out.

Buttons top-left switch what you see: SAR is the raw radar image, Segmentation highlights what the model found, Parcels shows the oil droplets being traced backwards, AIS tracks shows the ships.

3 · The slider under the map — running time backwards

This is the heart of it. Oil photographed at 05:42 was not dumped at 05:42 — it drifted. Drag the slider and you rewind the sea hour by hour, watching the oil travel back toward the point it was released.

Drift uncertainty added is how fuzzy the answer has become. Once it passes 12 km the hour goes grey and the word next to it changes to no — discarded. The system throws those hours away rather than guess. That refusal is deliberate and it is worth pointing at.

4 · The right column — the measurements and the suspects

Slick characterisation is the oil measured: how big, how long, how stretched out, which way it points, how much darker than clean sea. Look-alike discriminator shows the five checks that separate real oil from algae, rain and calm patches — longer bar means more oil-like.

Candidate vessels is the ranked answer. Click any ship to open its evidence: how many hours it sat inside the release corridor, how close it came, and whether its steering direction matches the direction the oil ribbon points. Ships marked excluded by model did not match the usable corridor and had full sampled coverage. Missing coverage is marked insufficient data.

Evidence weighting, just above that list, is the part to hand over. Those five sliders are the weights in the scoring formula. Move one and every score is recomputed and the list re-sorts in front of you, with the arithmetic printed underneath. Press Behaviour-led to explore how the ranking responds to stronger gap and type weights. An AIS gap can reflect reception issues; it does not establish intent. Nothing in this panel is a stored number.

Two things survive any weighting you can set. Ships without a corridor match stay excluded or unknown according to their coverage. The generated corridor itself is uncertain. And the recovery test below the map follows your weights honestly — weight it badly and it will tell you the true vessel is no longer first.

5 · Below the map — proof

Recovery test compares what really happened in the simulation against what the system worked out, without the system ever being told. Held-out test set is the model's accuracy on 300 images it never saw while training. Uncurated batch is the whole system's score across scenes nobody picked.

The one honest caveat

The model is trained on radar images generated from published physics, not on the labelled Krestenitis dataset, which sits behind a request form. Say this before anyone asks. The data loader swaps to the real dataset with one flag.

What this system cannot do

  • Synthetic data. The scenes, vessel traffic and environmental fields are simulated. Real-world accuracy has not been measured.
  • Retrospective analysis. This demonstration is not a continuous satellite-monitoring or alert service.
  • Uncertain attribution. Drift and AIS coverage can be insufficient. Excluded means outside this model's tested corridor, not proven innocent.
  • Manual evidence scores. Scores are ranking indices, not probabilities of responsibility. Changing weights can change the leading candidate.
  • Look-alikes and missed spills. Dark radar patches may have other causes. Misses and wrong rankings remain in the evaluation.
  • Upload assumptions. Image screening is a heuristic; physical measurements assume 80 m/pixel and a known display stretch. Uploads do not identify a vessel.
